## D1. Juno Pad moves from phase 2.1 to phase 2.2 (2026-11-13, phase 2.1)

The plan ships Juno Pad with the LFO. Its signature is pulse-width modulation from an LFO, and
an LFO reaches a destination only through the matrix, which is phase 2.2.

- **Move Juno Pad to 2.2 (taken, recommended).** It ships with the matrix, one chunk later,
  with the route the bank file will use for every later preset. No throwaway code.
- Add a hardwired "LFO1 to G1 pulse width" parameter now. Juno Pad ships an hour earlier, but
  the parameter is a dead end once the matrix exists and stays in the patch format.
- Ship Juno Pad without PWM. It would not sound like a Juno pad, which is the whole preset.

Changing it: nothing to undo; Juno Pad lands in 2.2 either way.

## D2. Tempo for synced LFOs: the host's, else the arp's internal tempo (2026-11-13, phase 2.1)

A synced LFO needs a tempo when the host gives none (the standalone never does).

- **Host tempo when the host reports one, otherwise the existing arp internal tempo parameter
  (taken, recommended).** One tempo for the arp and every synced modulator, so an arp and a
  synced wobble never drift apart. No new parameter.
- A separate part tempo parameter. More flexible, but two tempos that can disagree, and a
  second knob to set in every synced preset.
- Fixed 120 BPM without a host. Simple, but no way to set the standalone's tempo.

Changing it: give `PartSettings` its own tempo and read it in `Part::renderChunk`.

## D3. What the "16-step" LFO shape is (2026-11-13, phase 2.1)

The plan lists a 16-step shape without saying what it holds; the STEP modulator with editable
values is a separate source in phase 2.3.

- **A rising staircase of 16 equal levels per cycle (taken, recommended).** Predictable and
  useful on its own: through a pitch route it plays a stepped run, on a filter a stepped sweep.
  Bank name `steps`.
- A looping random 16-step pattern, new per note. Overlaps sample-and-hold and cannot be set.
- The STEP modulator's values. Couples two modulators and duplicates the STEP source.

Changing it: `LfoShape::steps` in `src/synth/v3/Lfo.h`.

## D4. LFO delay as well as fade-in (2026-11-13, phase 2.1)

The plan names a fade-in. Logic's and Halion's LFOs, and the Juno's, also have a delay before
the modulation starts (checked online: Logic Pro "Delay" and "Attack", Halion one-shot modes).

- **Delay plus fade-in (taken, recommended).** Delayed vibrato, the classic use, needs both. One
  more parameter per LFO (nine instead of eight).
- Fade-in only. Delayed vibrato would start rising at once.

Changing it: drop `delay` from the LFO group; presets then must not use it.

## D5. LFO behaviour details (2026-11-13, phase 2.1)

Smaller calls made while writing the class, each with what else was possible:

- **Free-running local LFOs advance only while their voice sounds (taken).** The alternative,
  advancing all 16 voices' LFOs all the time, costs CPU for no audible gain: with retrigger off
  the phase a note meets is arbitrary either way.
- **Delay and fade restart at every note even with retrigger off; retrigger decides only the
  phase (taken).** The alternative, no fade at all when free-running, loses the
  free-running-vibrato-that-fades-in patch.
- **A synced, free-running LFO follows the host's song position while the transport plays
  (taken).** The alternative, ignoring the song position, lets loops drift between passes.
- **Global LFOs default to synced and free-running; local ones to free rate and retrigger
  (taken).** Matches the design text ("tempo-synced, one phase shared by all voices"). The
  alternative was identical defaults everywhere.
- **Fade-in is a straight line (taken).** An exponential or S-curve fade was the alternative;
  the straight line is what the Juno and most plug-ins do and what the test checks.
- **Shape start points: sine and triangle at 0 rising, the saws and steps at their low end
  (saw down at its top), square high (taken).** The alternative, all shapes from their low
  point, makes sine and triangle start with a jump when routed to pitch.

## D6. How far a pitch route reaches (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

Matrix amounts run from -1 to 1. The design says pitch destinations are in semitones but not
what an amount of 1 means.

- **1 is 48 semitones (taken, recommended).** Four octaves cover the 808 drop (24), the sync
  sweep (24 to 36) and a riser; vibrato of a quarter tone is 0.005, still writable in a bank
  file. Matches the slot pitch span.
- 1 is 24 semitones. Finer for vibrato, but a three-octave sync sweep needs two slots.
- 1 is 96 semitones. Covers everything in one slot, but vibrato amounts get tiny.
- Amounts written in semitones for pitch slots. Most readable, but the amount parameter would
  have to know its slot's destination; a job for the real UI in phase 9.

Changing it: `matrixPitchSemitones` in `src/synth/v3/ModMatrix.h`; presets scale with it.

## D7. What the VCA destination does (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

- **Gain is 1 plus the offset, held between 0 and 1 (taken, recommended).** Negative amounts
  duck, a unipolar LFO with amount -1 gates (Outrun's Gated Pad), and modulation can never push
  a voice above its envelope, so headroom stays as measured. The gain ramps across each
  sub-block, so a square gate does not click (tested).
- Held between 0 and 2. Positive amounts could boost, but a voice could double in level and
  break the fixed headroom.
- An offset in decibels. Natural for tremolo, but a gate needs a very large negative amount.

Changing it: the VCA lines at the end of `Voice::renderSubBlock`.

## D8. Matrix curves (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

The design names linear, exponential and S-curve. Checked online: HALion and Waldorf split
sources into unipolar (0..1) and bipolar (-1..1); X-Stream offers logarithmic positive and
negative curves; Ultra and HISE multiply the amount by the aux (via) source.

- **Linear, Unipolar, Exponential, Logarithmic, S-Curve (taken, recommended).** Unipolar turns
  a bipolar LFO into 0..1, which gating and one-sided vibrato need; logarithmic is the inward
  curve next to the outward exponential. Curves keep the sign; the via source scales after.
- The design's three only. A unipolar LFO would need an extra slot with a constant offset,
  which the matrix has no source for.

Changing it: `ModCurve` and `applyCurve` in `ModMatrix.h`; add new curves only at the end.

## D9. Matrix destinations left for later (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

The design also lists envelope times and other matrix slots' amounts as destinations.

- **Defer both to phase 2.3 or later (taken, recommended).** Envelope times are read once per
  segment, so they need a note-on evaluation path, which comes naturally with the global
  envelope in 2.3. A slot amount as destination does what the via source already does.
  Destination numbers stay stable because new ones go at the end.
- Build them now. More work in 2.2 for no preset that needs them yet.

## D10. Juno Pad uses a global LFO (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

- **GLFO1, free-running, sweeping every voice's pulse width together (taken, recommended).**
  The Juno-106 has a single LFO for all voices (checked online), and the pad's shimmer comes

## D11. 808 Boom playing behaviour (2026-11-13, phase 2.2)

- **Legato with 70 ms glide on overlapping keys, both envelopes one-shot (taken,
  recommended).** Overlapping keys slide without restarting the boom (the trap 808 slide),
  detached keys hit again, and a short tap still booms for its full 1.4 s.
- Mono with retrigger. Every key restarts the boom; no slides.
- Poly. Booms stack into mud on fast notes.

The recipe's pitch drop (24 semitones in 60 ms) is the modern 808-bass punch; the original
TR-808 sweeps much less (one study of samples found a median under a semitone), checked online.

## D14. What opens and closes the global envelope (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **The keys as played, before the arpeggiator (taken, recommended).** It opens with the first
  key of a phrase (none other held) or, with trigger Every Note, with every key, retriggering
  from its current level. It closes when the last key and the pedal are up and no latched arp
  notes remain. A riser under an arp keeps rising while the chord is held.
- The voices: open while any voice sounds. A release tail would hold it open, and every arp
  step would count as a new note.
- The arp's notes. The envelope would restart on every step.

## D15. Synced global-envelope times (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **One set of millisecond times, read as at 120 BPM and scaled by 120 / tempo when sync is on;
  bank files may write `8bars` or `2beats` (taken, recommended).** One parameter per stage; at
  120 BPM a bar is 2000 ms. The generic editor shows the 120 BPM milliseconds until the real UI.
- Separate bar and beat parameters for each stage. Twice the parameters, two ways to set the
  same thing.
- Times always in beats when synced, with their own range. The same parameter would mean
  different units depending on another switch, worse for automation.

## D16. Step modulator details (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **Values -1..1; slide per step with one shared glide (the share of a step a slide takes);
  retrigger on by default (taken, recommended).** With retrigger on it restarts at the first
  note after silence, like the global LFOs, so a pattern starts on the first step in the
  standalone. Off, it follows the host's song position while the transport plays.
- Retrigger off by default. Patterns would sit on the song's grid in a DAW without a setting,
  but in the standalone each phrase would start on an arbitrary step.
- A glide time per step. Sixteen more parameters for a rarely needed difference.

## D17. The chaos source (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **A random walk: a new random target every 0.5 to 1.5 periods of its rate, through two
  smoothing stages; one parameter, the rate (taken, recommended).** Wanders without corners
  (largest step 0.0013 per sub-block at 1 Hz), bounded to -1..1.
- A chaotic attractor (Lorenz, logistic map). More "chaotic" in name, but it orbits a fixed
  shape and needs scaling per parameter set.
- A second "smoothness" parameter. S&H on a global LFO already gives the stepped version.

## D18. Macros are smoothed (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **Each macro moves toward its setting with a 10 ms time constant, per sub-block; the first
  block starts at the setting (taken, recommended).** A macro on a cutoff or level does not
  zipper when automated or turned.
- No smoothing. Host automation would step the destination once per block.

## D19. Envelope-time destinations read once per note (2026-11-13, phase 2.3)

- **Evaluated when a note starts or retriggers, from that note's velocity, key and random value
  and the part's current sources; an amount of 1 is 16 times longer (taken, recommended).** The
  uses are velocity and key to envelope time, which are per-note values anyway. No cost while
  the note plays.
- Continuous modulation, rescaling the running segment every sub-block. An LFO could wobble a
  decay, but it needs a segment rescale with an exponential per change.

## D22. How ping-pong works (2026-11-13, phase 2.4)

Checked online: the E-MU Proteus X manual and Steinberg's Multi Delay sum the input to mono into
the left line and cross-couple the two lines; pyo's example warns that a block-based version
runs the right side one block late.

- **Mono sum into the left line, the lines cross-coupled per sample through the same darkening
  feedback path as tape mode (taken, recommended).** Echoes land left at T, right at 2T, left at
  3T (tested), each bounce scaled by the feedback and a little darker, so ping-pong and tape
  sound like the same echo unit. Per-sample coupling has no block lag. With no feedback there
  is a single echo on the left.
- A clean, unfiltered feedback path for ping-pong. Brighter, but a second voice for the delay.
- Stereo cross-feedback without the mono sum (Retrologue's "Cross"). Keeps the input's stereo
  image but a centred source no longer bounces.

Changing mode clears the lines, since the two leave very different echoes behind.

## D25. How the noise generator makes its four kinds (2026-11-13, phase 3.1)

Checked online: the NESdev wiki and emulator sources give the NES noise channel as a 15-bit
shift register with feedback from bit 1 (32767 steps) or bit 6 (93 steps, "short mode"); the
musicdsp pink-noise page notes that fixed pinking coefficients degrade at higher sample rates.

- **Pink from seven first-order shelves designed at the running sample rate, poles every two
  octaves from 10 Hz, zeros an octave above (taken, recommended).** Measured within 0.67 dB per
  octave from 62 Hz to 16 kHz at the 96 kHz voice rate. The alternatives: Paul Kellet's fixed
  coefficients (made for 44.1 kHz, they drift at 96 kHz), or Voss-McCartney (cheap, but rippled).
- **Band noise: a state-variable band-pass on the note, its gain set from the resonance and the
  note so the level stays even (taken).** Measured within 0.4 dB from 200 Hz wide to 3 kHz narrow.
  Without the compensation a high, narrow band would be 20 dB quieter than a low, wide one.
- **Chip: the register clocked at 93 times the note, so Short mode plays the note; Clock moves it
  in semitones like the NES period divider; every register step band-limited with the VA's
  4-point BLEP (taken, recommended).** A Short register at 375 Hz repeats every 256 samples to
  within 0.001. A naive register aliases badly once the clock passes the sample rate. Keeping
  the aliasing as "chip character" was the alternative; the crusher (4.2) can add it on purpose.
- **Every kind sits about 3 dB under a saw at the same slot level (taken).** Noise sounds louder
  than a saw at equal RMS, and the presets set their own levels anyway.

## D29. Link errors on the embedded bank library (2026-11-13, phases 3.1 to 4.2)

Corrected a second time in phase 4.2, below the original entry: the cause is the incremental
linker, not CMake reconfiguring. After 06-basement.csbank was added, the failure came back even
with the library deleted and built on its own first; deleting the `.ilk` files fixed it, and
every failure had followed a new bank file (a new library member). Taken: incremental linking
off in Debug (`/INCREMENTAL:NO`; little to gain with an optimised build), the TODO note now says
to delete the `.ilk` files if it ever returns. The alternative, keeping incremental linking and
deleting `.ilk` files by hand after each new bank file, was rejected as a trap. The original
entry follows.


The embedded bank library failed to link four times (LNK1236, once LNK1106). Every time, CMake
had reconfigured itself in the middle of the build, which a new bank file or a CMakeLists change
triggers. That build can write the library truncated, and later builds update the broken archive
in place, so they keep failing. Deleting `build/Debug/CoolSynthFactoryBank.lib` and building
again always fixed it. (Corrected on the second occurrence: at first I blamed a stale .pdb.)

- **Configure explicitly after adding a bank file (`cmake --preset vs2022-debug`), and delete the
  library if the error appears; both written in TODO's standing notes (taken, recommended).**
- Delete the library before every build with a pre-build step. Never fails, but every build
  would relink the plugin, the standalone and the tests, even with nothing changed.
- Embed the bank in the plugin's own sources instead of a JUCE binary-data library. Removes the
  library, but replaces JUCE's generator with one of our own.

The data-only libraries now also build without debug info, which they never needed.

## D30. The two-operator FM generator (2026-11-13, phase 3.2)

- **A type of its own, "FM2", with the four-operator generator a separate type in 3.3 (taken,
  recommended).** Two operators fit the eight slot parameters with room to spare (ratio, fine,
  index, feedback, carrier ratio, mix); four operators need paging. One FM4 type for both was the
  alternative, but a two-operator patch would carry two unused operators and the paging.
- **Ratio is continuous (0.25 to 16, logarithmic), not stepped to DX ratios (taken).** Integer and
  half ratios are typed exactly in bank files, and a sweep of the ratio through the matrix
  makes the inharmonic glides that stepped ratios cannot.
- **Feedback through the average of the modulator's last two outputs (taken).** Reported online
  (Native Instruments forum, on emulating Yamaha chips; the thread itself calls it unproven) as
  what keeps high feedback from buzzing at half the sample rate. Measured: full feedback leaves
  -72 dB above 40 kHz.
- **Phases restart at a fresh note and run in double precision (taken).** Every attack is the
  same, as on a DX; free-running phases would make each note's attack differ.

## D31. Keeping FM from aliasing (2026-11-13, phase 3.2)

Checked online: CCRMA's FM notes suggest limiting the index to about (fs/2 - carrier) /
modulator; forum advice is to oversample.

- **Limit the index where the first sideband that would fold into the audible band, after the
  voice's decimation, has a Bessel amplitude under 1e-4 (taken, recommended).** Measured: the
  largest alias below 20 kHz -75 dB against the strongest sideband at a 2 kHz carrier with index
  10 asked for (held at 3.0). Low and middle notes are never limited.
- The Carson-rule cap from CCRMA. Tried first: -57.6 dB, since the Bessel tail just past
  Carson's bandwidth still folds back.
- Oversample the FM generator again internally. Clean at any index, but twice the cost and a
  second decimator per voice.
- No limit. DX-style grit at high notes, but uncontrolled.

## D33. The four-operator FM generator (2026-11-13, phase 3.3)

The plan asks for six algorithms with "per-operator level and ratio across P1..P8 with paging".

- **Eight parameters, no paging: algorithm, the ratios of operators 2 to 4, their levels and
  feedback on operator 4; operator 1 is the carrier at the note (taken, recommended).** Paging
  needs UI support the throwaway editor does not have; the slot's own octave, semitone and fine
  move operator 1. Per-operator fine detune is the price; a ratio like 14.02 still detunes one.
- Paging over two pages of eight. More control, but the generic editor would show sixteen
  generic parameters for one slot and the bank syntax would need pages.
- **Six algorithms: stack, two into one, stack plus one, two pairs, one into three, organ
  (taken).** They cover the classic four-operator families (the DX7's 32 six-operator algorithms
  reduce to these shapes on four operators).
- **A Level is the index (1 is 8 radians) where the operator modulates and its output level where
  it sounds; carriers are summed and normalised by their total level (taken).** One knob per
  operator whatever the algorithm; the output never exceeds 1 (tested on all six at full).
- **Every modulation is limited with the two-operator generator's alias rule for the operator it
  drives (taken).**

Grid Keys (Outrun) ships on the Two Pairs algorithm: a 1:1 body with velocity bark and a 1:14
tine that rings for 250 ms.

## D34. The factory wavetables (2026-11-13, phase 3.4)

The plan asks for an embedded factory set: classic, digital, vocal, chip 4-bit.

- **Four tables of 16 frames, computed from their spectra at first use, not stored as audio
  files (taken, recommended).** No third-party tables to license, nothing in the repository but
  code, and every frame is exact. Classic blends sine, triangle, saw and square spectrum by
  spectrum with the fundamental in phase (no thin spot mid-morph); Digital is a hard-synced saw

## D35. Band-limited playback (2026-11-13, phase 3.4)

## D38. The state-variable filter (2026-11-13, phase 4.1)

- **Andy Simper's trapezoidal SVF, 12 dB per octave, LP, HP, band-pass at a peak gain of 1 and
  notch; resonance from Q 0.5 to Q 40, growing exponentially (taken, recommended).** The SVF is

  wind. At Q 40 it rings long but always decays (tested); self-oscillation stays the ladder's job.

  the point of this type.

## D61. The modal generator's resonators (2026-11-13, phase 6.2)

- **Twelve rotating complex resonators, z = (z + x) r e^(j theta) (taken, recommended).** The
  ring is a decaying sine of exactly the set frequency and 60 dB time (measured: every partial

## D62. What Wood, Metal and Glass are (2026-11-13, phase 6.2)

Checked online: the free-free bar's modes are 1 : 2.76 : 5.40 : 8.93 (BYU acoustics, "Free-Free
Bar"; University of Colorado PHYS 1240 lab handout); measured ratios of tuned bars and a wine
glass are in the Csound manual's "Modal Frequency Ratios" table (compiled by John Bower for
Scott Lindroth), which also warns that some sets only sound right in their own register.

- **Wood is a tuned (undercut) bar, 1 : 3.93 : 9.54 : 16.7; Metal a uniform bar, 1 : 2.76 :
  5.40 : 8.93; Glass a wine glass, 1 : 2.32 : 4.25 : 6.63 : 9.38 (taken, recommended).** A
  marimba sounds like one because its second partial is tuned two octaves up; a glockenspiel
  bar is close to uniform. Between two materials every ratio moves geometrically, which between
  Wood and Metal is what cutting a bar's arch deeper does. The measured rows give five or six
  ratios; the rest follow their trend and are marked so in the code.
- Wood as the uniform bar and Metal as a plate. My first draft: its "plate" ratios turned out to
  be a drum membrane's (the zeros of Bessel functions), and a uniform bar with wooden damping is
  a wood block, not a marimba.
- One inharmonicity control from a string to a bar, as Rings has. A continuous sweep, but no
  position on it is a tuned bar.
- **Each material also sets how fast upper partials die: a partial at ratio q rings q^-e times
  as long as the fundamental, e = 1.4 for wood, 0.5 for metal, 0.8 for glass, and Damping adds
  up to 1.2 (taken).** Measured 200 ms after a hard strike: the second partial is 38.6 dB under
  the fundamental on wood and 6.9 dB under on metal.

## D65. Where chord memory sits and how it keeps track (2026-11-13, phase 7.1)

- **A stage of its own in front of the arpeggiator that rewrites one chunk's events: every key
  remembers the notes it started, and a count per note says how many keys hold it (taken,
  recommended).** A release ends exactly what that key started, even if the chord type changed
  while it was down or chord memory was switched off; a note two keys share is struck again by
  the second and ends with the last. The arpeggiator, the play modes and the allocator are
  untouched: they see keys.
- Expanding inside the allocator. Fewer events, but the arp would not see the chord, which the
  requirement asks for, and mono and unison would each need their own chord handling.
- **The global envelope still follows the keys as played (taken).** The part merges three
  streams in time order (arp steps, chord notes, played keys); a key is tracked just before the
  first event it caused. With chord memory off the order of every call is what it was (the
  existing note-layer tests pass unchanged). Alternative: let the envelope follow the chord's
  notes, which with "every note" and a strum would restart it on every strummed note.
- **A key struck again without a release keeps the notes both chords share and lets the others
  go (taken).** With chord memory off that is one note-on, as before.

- **Learning is a switch, `pA.chord.learn` (taken).** While it is on, keys play plain notes and

  the throwaway UI does not have.

## D77. The factory samples are made by the program (2026-11-13, phase 8)

- **Five samples generated at first use: Glass (a bowed wine glass), Choir, Strings, Air and
  Harp; each four seconds of mono at 48 kHz, tuned to C4, levelled alike, closed into a
  seamless loop, and kept at three band limits (20, 10 and 5 kHz) (taken, recommended).**

  of milliseconds once per process and 11.5 MB of memory shared by every instance. The loop is
  made by folding the half second after the end into the start, so a loop over the whole sample
  and a grain crossing the end never click. The band levels let a sample play up to four
  octaves above C4 without audible aliasing.

- Glass follows what a rubbed glass does (checked online: a singing glass is driven by stick and
  slip like a bowed string, Jundt et al., "Vibrational modes of partly filled wine glasses",
  UNSW; its free modes are 1 : 2.32 : 4.25, Csound modal table): a nearly pure sustained tone
  with weak harmonics, a slow beating as the pattern follows the finger, a faint ring of the
  free modes and a breath of friction noise.

## D78. The sample generator (2026-11-13, phase 8)

- **Sample, Start, Loop, Loop Start, Loop End, Loop Fade, Reverse (taken).** C4 is the sample's
  own speed; the slot's key tracking switch gives "pitch tracking off". A loop fades what lies
  before Loop Start in under the last part of the loop (up to half of it), equal power; measured
  on a 0.2 s loop of Glass, the largest step is 0.56 without the fade and 0.0106 with it, which
  is the sample's own largest step. A one-shot fades out over its last 10 ms. The read blends
  two band levels by how far the note transposes the sample.
- A fade that needs room before the loop start in the file. Not needed: the samples are loops,
  so there is always something before any point.
- User samples. The requirement puts import later; it needs a file dialog, a place in the state
  and a loader off the audio thread.

## D79. The grain generator (2026-11-13, phase 8)

Checked online: grain clouds are levelled by a gain that compensates the overlap of the grains
and their window (Riedel et al., "Perceptual evaluation of listener envelopment using spatial
granular synthesis", arXiv 2301.10210), commonly the square root of the number of active grains;
"spray" is the usual name for a random offset of each grain's read position.

- **Up to twelve grains per voice from a factory sample; Position, Scan, Size, Density, Spray,
  Pitch Spray, Spread (taken, recommended).** The level follows one over the root of the
  overlap (measured: 0.093, 0.121 and 0.149 RMS for overlaps of 1, 5 and 12). The window is
  (4x(1-x)) squared, within a few percent of a Hann window at three multiplications a sample.
  Spray scatters the read position up to a second either way and loosens the timing by up to
  half an interval; at 0 the grains fall on a grid, which is a sound of its own.
- **Freeze is Scan at 0 (taken).** The eight parameters are used up; Scan runs from -2 to 2
  times the sample's speed and its middle is the frozen cloud.
- **No grains from another generator yet. Review: the requirement names it.** Each voice would
  need about a second of memory per generator slot to record into, and slots are sized for
  their largest type: several megabytes per voice across 32 voices. It needs a buffer pool the
  part hands out, which is a piece of work of its own.
- More grains per voice. Twelve already cost 14 % of real time for eight notes in this build;
  thicker clouds come from longer grains.

## D80. Granular Sky (2026-11-13, phase 8)

- **A frozen cloud on Glass (40 grains a second of 250 ms, Pitch Spray at 5 cents, wide), a
  thinner cloud an octave up that drifts through the sample, and the sample itself quietly
  underneath, through a low-pass into the long plate (taken).** The recipe asks for "density
  high": 40 a second of this length already keeps ten grains sounding, near the limit of twelve.

## D98. How the sampled instruments are built (2026-11-13)

Carries out D96 and D97. Choices inside the build:

- **The Sample generator plays them: its Sample parameter lists the five generated samples and
  then every instrument, and an eighth parameter is the Style (taken, recommended).** One
  generator, so a recorded instrument can be mixed, filtered and modulated like any other
  source, three to a voice. A separate "Instrument" generator type would have doubled the code
  for the same job.
- **A note takes the recording nearest its pitch (the lower of two equally near), the dynamic
  layer its velocity falls in, and the next take in turn; it is read at the speed that gives
  the note's pitch (taken).** No crossfade between neighbouring recordings or layers: two
  recordings of one note are not in phase, and mixing them thins the tone.
- **Reading is a 16-point windowed sinc with 256 phases, interpolated, at three cutoffs for
  notes played above their recording (taken, recommended).** The four-point Hermite read the
  generated samples use is cheaper and audibly duller and less clean on a piano two octaves
  up; quality comes first (the user's rule).
- **Sustaining styles get a loop from the builder (taken): over the steady part of each
  recording, crossfaded for up to half a second, its end moved to where the wave best matches
  the start.** Checked on 9 s notes of cello and organ: no step in level, no click. Struck and
  plucked styles ring out as recorded. Hand-set loops would be better still and are 900 files
  of work; no loops would end a held string note after a few seconds.
- **One gain per style, applied as it is read, brings the style's loudest recording to 0.9 of
  full scale (taken).** The balance between an instrument's notes and dynamics stays as
  recorded. Normalising every file would flatten that balance; no gain at all leaves the pipe
  organ 27 dB below the piano. Whether notes should be evened out across the keyboard is open:
  see D99.
- **The library is looked for in the folder named in `COOLSYNTH_LIBRARY`, else the one written
  in `CoolSynth/library.path` in the user's application data, else `CoolSynth/Library` in the
  common application data (taken).** An installer writes the second; the first is for testing.
- **Memory: at most 3 GB of instruments; one nothing has asked for in a minute and a half goes
  first (taken).** A note asked for before its instrument is in memory is silent, never half an
  instrument; the next note sounds.
- **A name need only be unique within its room (taken for now; the user is asked).** The
  Greenhouse has a modelled Marimba and Kalimba, the Conservatory the recorded ones. Program
  names carry the room ("Conservatory: Mallets and Bells: Marimba"), and the render tool takes
  "Room: Name". The options: this; rename the models ("Marimba Bar", "Kalimba Tine", as "Harp
  String" already is); or rename the recordings.
- **The loudness tool plays a sampled instrument in the middle of the notes it was recorded
  at (taken).** At the tool's usual C4 chord a flute is below its lowest note and a double bass
  at its top.
- **Tests never read the real library (taken).** A stand-in is written to a temporary folder:
  every style of the catalogue as five quarter-second sine recordings with two layers, two
  takes and a loop. One small FLAC file in `tests/data` checks the FLAC path.

## D99. Faults found in the library as first built (2026-11-13)

The first build places each recording by the note in its file name, moved by whole octaves to
where a pitch detector hears it. Checked afterwards against the mapping files the library's
author publishes (github.com/sgossner/VSCO-2-CE, branch SFZ) and by measuring partials:

- The horn sits an octave too high (the author: `MOHorn_sus_A0` is A1; the detector caught
  the second harmonic of most notes). Tubular Bells A sits two octaves too high and B probably
  one (a bell's strike note is not its lowest partial). The Renaissance organ's 4' stop is
  placed by its sound, an octave above its key, and so no longer sounds an octave up.
- The timpani files are numbered drums, not named notes, and the detector spread each drum's
  hits over several wrong notes. The drums are also between notes as recorded (the first
  set: F#2, B2 -21 cents, C#3 +48, E3 +44, G3 -42).
- The didgeridoo folder is three drones, five phrases and four calls, not notes.
- A flute's low C is 20 dB below its top C as recorded, and neighbouring recordings differ by
  up to 3 dB. The author's own mapping evens levels out by hand, per recording.

To be corrected in the builder next: the octave given per style where a detector cannot be
trusted; drums and unnamed sets grouped and placed by their measured main partial; a tuning
correction per recording for sets that are not in tune; and, the user's choice, whether levels
are evened out across the keyboard.

## D100. The library is built from the author's own mapping files (2026-11-13)

Correcting the faults of D99 turned up what makes them moot: the recordings' author publishes
his instruments as SFZ mapping files, 258 of them (github.com/sgossner/VCSL branch `sfz`,
github.com/sgossner/VSCO-2-CE branch `SFZ`; `tools/audition/fetch_sfz.py` downloads them). For
each recording they give the note it sounds, the keys and the velocities that play it, a level
correction and often a tuning correction, and for each instrument a release time and whether
loudness follows velocity. A note in the Steinway's folder says its dynamic layers are
levelled alike and need a velocity curve to get their dynamics back. The first build knew
none of this: it guessed notes from file names and a pitch detector, split velocities evenly,
and played level-matched layers through an envelope that gave them 6 dB of dynamics.

- **The builder takes notes, keys, velocities and levels from the author's mapping files
  (taken, recommended).** 74 of the 76 styles have one. Every fault of D99 goes with it: the
  horn is A1 to F5, the tubular bells C4 to F5, the timpani are five drums on five notes.
  Keeping my own mapping and correcting it by hand where it was found wrong would leave the
  faults nobody found. The Old Trombone has no mapping file and is still placed by its file
  names, its layers splitting the velocities evenly and its recordings levelled alike.
- **The Sample generator plays a sampled instrument with the law those levels are made for:
  level = velocity squared, as far as the instrument follows velocity at all (not at all for
  the organs and harpsichords) (taken).** The Conservatory presets switch the amplifier
  envelope's own velocity off. The alternative, the envelope's straight-line law, gives a
  piano whose softest note is 6 dB below its loudest, or needs every preset to undo the
  author's levels.
- **A zone is the keys and velocities the mapping gives a recording; a note outside every zone
  takes the nearest recorded note (taken).** Where the author crossfades two dynamic layers
  (marimba, folk harp, glockenspiel, the second timpani) the layers switch at the middle of
  the fade: D98 stands, two recordings of a note are not mixed.
- **Tuning is measured here, not taken from the mapping files (taken).** Their corrections
  come from a tuner that is steady on wind and string tones and wanders on drums and bars:
  six hits of one kettledrum get corrections from -12 to +78 cents, while its main partial is
  steady within 2. Measured here per recording from the first eight harmonics (winds,
  strings, brass, organs: within 1 to 6 cents of the author's values where he gives any), from
  the first partial alone (bars, tines, glasses: xylophone and glockenspiel are 15 cents
  sharp as recorded, the kalimba up to 50 off its notes), and per drum from its main partial.
  Left as recorded: the pianos and harpsichords (stretched on purpose), the tubular bells (a
  bell has no partial at its note) and the three TX81Z patches (exact as they are). Taking
  the author's corrections is a builder option (`tune=author`) and is not used.
- **An instrument sounds the key that is played, also where the author maps it otherwise
  (taken; the user is asked).** His mappings put the xylophone an octave and the
  glockenspiels an octave below where they sound, as they are written; the TX81Z electric
  piano sounds an octave below its key and the Clavisynth an octave above (his note says so);
  the tubular glockenspiel's file names are not its pitches. All are moved to where they
  sound (`shift=` in the catalogue). The other option: leave them as the author mapped them,
  as a player of the written part expects.
- **The didgeridoo is three styles, not the author's kit (taken).** His mapping puts its twelve
  recordings on twelve keys. Here the three drones are one pitched, looped instrument on C#2,
  and the phrases and the calls are styles of their own.
- **The second timpani set is the author's "Scale" mapping (taken):** 54 of its 204 hits,
  chosen to make a scale. That mapping is hand-made and not consistent with itself (one
  drum's two layers are given pitches 135 cents apart), so each drum's pitch is measured and
  the keys stay his. Four of its nine drum tunings differ from his by more than 40 cents;
  the user's ear decides.
- The release of each Conservatory preset is the author's (0.4 s for the grand, 0.8 s for
  bowed strings, 15 to 30 s for mallets, bells, harp and timpani, which ring on after the key
  as the instruments do; the envelope allows 20 s), and its attack is 2 ms: the recording
  brings its own.
- **Levels (taken).** Recordings levelled alike by the author do not peak alike, so a style's
  gain puts the middle one of its loudest notes at half of full scale instead of its single
  highest peak at 0.9 (which left whole instruments 10 dB down). A preset has one level and
  an instrument several styles, so each style after the first carries a trim, measured by the
  loudness tool (`CoolSynthPresetNormalizer --styles`, written to the catalogue by
  `apply_trims.py`): -4.4 dB for the bowed vibraphone, +5 dB for the lower recorders; after
  it every style needs the same preset level within 0.1 dB. The loudness tool leaves a
  sampled preset room for fortissimo: its test chord at velocity 100 may peak at -6.2 dB, so
  the same chord at full velocity stays under the output's soft clipper. At a normal touch
  the pianos, mallets and harp therefore sit about 10 dB under the synth presets, bowed and
  blown instruments 3 to 6 dB under (the user is asked whether that is wanted).
- The demonstrations on the audition page (`make_demos.py`) are measured by `check_demos.py`:
  every style gets louder with velocity (26 dB from velocity 45 to 120 on the grand), notes
  are in tune within 5 cents in the middle of each style, and no file peaks above -1.5 dB.
- The library after the rebuild: 2,216 zones in 76 styles, 2,213 files, 1,393 MB;
  `verify_library.py` compared 2,213 files with their recordings, none different.

## D101. Harp String, second try: a finger's pluck (2026-11-13)

The user found neither version of Harp String close to the recorded harp (D94). Measured
harmonic by harmonic against the same recording (VCSL Concert Harp, nine notes A2 to E5), the
reason is plain: the recorded harp is dark, mostly its fundamental and third harmonic, its
sixth to tenth harmonics 30 to 80 dB down, with almost nothing above the twelfth even in the
attack. The preset's pluck was a burst of low-passed noise: at its brightness setting the
harmonics up to the tenth were within 20 dB of the strongest, different on every note, and
nearly all of the first 30 ms lay above the twelfth harmonic. The pluck position and decay
corrected in D91 were not what was wrong.

- **Harp String uses the string generator's Click excitation, a smooth pulse, made wider:
  half a period at Pick 0, a twentieth at Pick 1, at equal energy (taken, recommended).** A
  raised-cosine pulse a quarter to half a period wide has the recorded harp's fall of
  harmonics. No preset used Click, so nothing else changes; the pulse was at most a quarter
  period wide before.
- Keep the noise pluck and give it a steeper filter: it would change what Pick means for every
  string preset, and a noise pluck is still different on every note, which a finger is not.
- Drop the model and point the preset at the recorded Folk Harp: the Greenhouse is the room
  of modelled instruments, and the Conservatory already has the recording.
- The preset: Pick 0.3 at middle C, falling with the key (the recording's pluck is softer the
  higher the string), rising a little with velocity; Pick Position 0.44; Damping 0.3; Decay
  8 s at middle C, longer in the bass (key to Decay -0.5); a 12 ms breath of noise for the
  finger. Against the recording the first, third and fifth harmonics are now within 1 dB in
  the middle of the nine notes (11.5 dB before), and their levels a second later within 5 dB
  (10 dB before). Still different: the recording has more above the twelfth harmonic in its
  first 30 ms (by 3 to 17 dB), and its upper harmonics beat.
- It is on the audition page as "Harp String, second try": the recording, the version the
  user rejected, and this one. The user's ear decides.
